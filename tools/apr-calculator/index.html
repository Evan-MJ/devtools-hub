How the APR Is Worked Out

A lender does not guess the APR. It looks for the monthly rate that makes your loan add up exactly: the present value of every payment you make, plus the charges you paid to get the loan, has to equal the amount you borrowed. That rate is solved from the cash flows, and the annual percentage rate is that monthly rate times twelve, which is the monthly rate approximation the regulation allows.

  1. Amount financed is the cash you received, before any fees.
  2. Lender fees are added to the cost of the loan, because they are a credit charge whether you pay them on day one or spread them across five years.
  3. Payment and term give the full set of cash flows the lender works with.
  4. Monthly rate is solved so that the payments and the fees equal the amount financed.
  5. APR is the monthly rate times twelve, and the interest rate is the rate the payment alone implies, without the fees.

Take the default example on this page: 24,000 dollars borrowed, 600 dollars of lender charges, a 470 dollar monthly payment and 60 months. The payment on its own works out at an interest rate of about 6.54% a year. Once the 600 dollars of charges are folded in, the same payment works out at an APR of about 7.60%. The fee uplift is 1.06 percentage points, and it is not a rounding error: the loan costs 4,800 dollars over five years, 4,200 of it interest and 600 of it charges, and the disclosed APR is the rate that says so. The charges do not turn into interest in the payments you signed for, they simply mean you paid 4,800 dollars for 4,200 dollars of borrowing.

Run it the other way and a 24,000 dollar loan at 7.00% over 60 months has a payment of 475.23 dollars, 4,513.73 dollars of interest in total, and an APR of 8.07% once the same 600 dollars of charges are added. Two lenders can quote the same payment on the same loan and still land on different APRs, because one of them charges 600 dollars and the other charges none.

APR Is Not the Interest Rate

The interest rate prices the money itself. The APR adds the cost of getting the money and expresses the whole thing as a yearly rate, so two offers can be compared on one number. A loan at 6.0% interest with 400 dollars of charges over three years can carry a higher APR than a loan at 6.5% interest with no fees at all, because the fees are spread over a long term.

APR and APY are different animals. APY is the yearly return on an investment, or the yearly cost of a credit card where the interest compounds and the fee is usually a flat annual charge, so a 24% APR card is close to a 27% APY card. A loan APR is not compounded the same way, which is why lenders quote one and investors quote the other.

Which Costs Belong in the APR

Anything the lender charges for arranging the credit belongs in the APR: origination fees, documentation fees, points and processing charges. Amounts paid to a government do not, which covers sales tax, title and registration fees. This calculator keeps the two apart on purpose: lender charges raise the rate, and the government fees land in the all-in cost so nothing disappears from the total.

The same split applies to a car. A dealer who rolls the tax and the doc fee into the amount financed raises your payment and your APR at the same time, even though only part of that is a credit charge. Price the car at two dealers with the same rate and the higher price always produces the higher payment, which is why the same car can be financed at two very different APRs.

Comparing Offers Without Fooling Yourself

The comparison table gives you the monthly payment, the APR, the total interest and the total cost for every offer, and it marks the cheapest one over the whole term. That last column is the one that matters when you are picking between a 60 month loan at 6.5% and a 72 month loan at 6.0%. The longer loan has the better-looking rate and the worse bill, because you are paying interest for a year longer. If you are certain you will sell the car or pay the loan off early, a longer term can still win on cash flow, so look at the payment column as well as the total.

Why AI Chatbots Get This Wrong

A language model will quote you the note rate, add the fees to the payment instead of solving for the rate that makes them disappear into it, and then call the result the APR. It also tends to treat a government tax as a credit charge, which inflates the rate, and it cannot hold three offers in front of you while you change one field at a time. This page solves the cash flow equation with the same method a lender uses, shows the fee uplift in percentage points and dollars, and keeps tax out of the rate where the regulation keeps it out.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an APR?

The annual percentage rate is the yearly cost of a loan expressed as a percentage, with the charges you pay to get the loan folded into the rate. A 20,000 dollar loan at 6% interest over five years with 500 dollars of charges has a disclosed APR of about 7.06%, because the charges are spread across the life of the loan instead of being paid once at the start.

How do I calculate the APR on a car loan?

Add the amount you borrowed and the lender charges such as the documentation fee, then find the monthly rate that makes your monthly payment worth exactly that total over the loan term. This calculator solves that equation directly, so enter the amount financed, the charges, your monthly payment and the number of months and the APR comes back.

Why is my APR higher than the interest rate?

Because the disclosed rate includes the one-off charges. If you borrow 20,000 dollars over 60 months and pay 400 dollars of origination fees, those fees are spread over five years, so they add to the yearly rate even though you paid them on day one. The difference between the two numbers is the fee uplift in percentage points.

Which fees are included in the APR?

Charges the lender makes for arranging the credit are included, such as origination fees, documentation fees, points and processing charges. Things paid to a government, including sales tax, title and registration fees, are not part of the APR, so this calculator keeps them in the total cost but leaves them out of the rate.

Is a lower APR always the better loan?

No. A longer term usually carries a lower APR but costs more in total interest, because you are borrowing for longer. The comparison table shows the APR and the total cost side by side so you can see when the lowest rate is not the cheapest loan.

How is APR different from APY?

The yearly cost of borrowing, charges included, is what lenders quote for loans, while APY is the yearly yield on an investment or the yearly cost of a credit card, where interest compounds and the charges are usually a flat annual fee. A credit card APR of 24% is much closer to a 27% APY once daily compounding is taken into account.

Does the APR change if I pay the loan off early?

The disclosed APR assumes the loan runs to the end of its term, so it does not change when you pay it off early. Paying early does cut the total interest, and in most states a prepayment penalty on a personal loan can be charged as a separate cost, which this calculator treats as one of the fees.

Will the lender show the same APR as this calculator?

Usually to the last decimal place. Lenders work from the same cash flows under the Truth in Lending Act, but they apply the monthly rate approximation set out in the regulation, so a small difference of a few hundredths is normal. The number on your loan agreement is the one that binds.

Does this calculator upload my loan details?

No. Every figure is calculated in your browser with plain JavaScript, and nothing you type is sent to a server or stored. You can turn off your connection and the calculator keeps working.
