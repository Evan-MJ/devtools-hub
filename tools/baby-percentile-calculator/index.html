How This Baby Percentile Calculator Works

Every well-child visit ends with a number and a curve. This calculator takes your baby's weight, length or head circumference and places it on the WHO Child Growth Standards, the same reference your pediatrician or health visitor checks against. You enter the measurement and the age in months, and the tool solves the exact LMS equation behind the curve, so you get the percentile and the z-score rather than a rough estimate read off a picture. Since the reference tables load with the page and the math runs entirely in your browser, your numbers stay on your device.

The WHO standards were built from the Multicentre Growth Reference Study, which followed 8,440 children across Brazil, Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the United States and found that healthy growth is strikingly similar across all six. That is the reason the same curves are used by the CDC for children under two and by health services around the world. WHO publishes the curve parameters (L, M and S for each age and sex) and this calculator is driven directly by those published values, sampled at every WHO month point and interpolated linearly between them.

How to take the measurements

  1. Weight: undressed, before a feed, on the same scale each time.
  2. Length (until age 2): measured lying down on a firm surface, legs straight, heels together. Standing height from 24 months, and the WHO reference changes at that boundary.
  3. Head circumference: with a soft tape around the widest part, just above the eyebrows and ears, over the back of the head. Take two tries and use the larger.

Why AI Chatbots Cannot Replace This Tool

Ask a chatbot whether your 6-month-old's 7.9 kg is normal and you will get a friendly paragraph that may invent a benchmark with no reference table behind it. A growth percentile is not free-form knowledge: it is the exact result of a formula z = ((x / M) ^ L − 1) / (L × S), where L, M and S are World Health Organization curve parameters published in printed tables, one row per age and sex. Language models do not carry those tables reliably and often quote percentiles for the wrong age, the wrong sex, or the wrong reference. This calculator keeps the WHO tables in the page, interpolates them with the official convention of 30.4375 days per month and applies the published math exactly, so the same measurement always yields the same defensible percentile you can bring to a pediatric appointment.

How to Use It

  1. Choose the sex curve: Boy or Girl.
  2. Enter the age in months. For a baby born early, pick the gestational weeks and the corrected age is applied for you.
  3. Enter any of the three measurements in kg/cm or lb/in. At least one value is required.
  4. Press Calculate Percentiles to get the WHO percentile and z-score for each number you entered, plus the weight-for-length result when both weight and length are present.
  5. Use the chart select to see the measurement plotted between the 3rd and 97th percentile curves, and copy the summary text for your appointment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do baby growth percentiles actually mean?

A percentile is a position in a reference population, not a grade. A weight at the 30th percentile means about 30 of every 100 babies of the same age and sex in the reference group weigh less, and 70 weigh more. Every band on the growth chart is occupied by healthy babies by construction, so the percentile itself does not tell you whether your baby is doing well. What matters over time is where your baby travels along their own curve, which a pediatrician reads at regular visits.

Which growth chart does this calculator use?

This calculator uses the WHO Child Growth Standards (2006, with the head circumference curves released in 2007) for ages 0 to 24 months. Those are the curves recommended for infants in the United States and used in the NHS red book and by pediatric societies worldwide. The CDC 2000 charts take over from age 2, which is outside the range of this tool.

My baby is premature - should I use adjusted age?

Yes. For babies born more than a couple of weeks early, pediatric practice adjusts age by the number of weeks before 40 weeks of gestation, usually until 24 months of age. Our percentile calculator subtracts (40 − gestational weeks) from your baby's calendar age before solving the curves, so a baby born at 32 weeks who is 4 calendar months old is assessed at roughly a 2-month equivalent. Pick the gestational age from the dropdown and the corrected age is applied automatically.

My baby is on the 15th percentile - is that bad?

Most likely that is simply where your baby sits in the queue, and it is a perfectly healthy place to be as long as your baby stays roughly there over time. Only a small share of babies sit near the 50th percentile. A single measurement is a snapshot, and pediatricians look for a consistent trend, crossing of two major percentile lines, or a big mismatch between weight and length percentiles before anything else.

How is the percentile calculated?

From the published WHO LMS parameters. For your baby's exact age in months and sex, the calculator takes the three curve parameters L (a power that removes skew), M (the median) and S (a coefficient of variation), and computes a z-score as z = ((x / M) ^ L − 1) / (L × S). The z-score is then converted to a percentile with the standard normal distribution, and fractional ages are handled by interpolating linearly between WHO month points.

When is a percentile worth calling the pediatrician about?

The percentile describes position, not health, and no chart can know your baby's history. Call your pediatrician if your baby is below the 2nd or above the 98th percentile, if the curve crosses two major lines in either direction between visits, or if weight and length percentiles drift far apart over time. Those are the situations where a professional should read the pattern.

Do you store or upload the measurements?

Never. The WHO reference tables load with the page and every calculation runs inside your browser. Your measurements are not sent anywhere, not saved and do not appear in any server log; open the network tab while you calculate and you will see no outgoing requests.

Why does a weight-for-length row appear when I enter both weight and length?

Weight-for-length is the other way pediatric charts express a baby's size: it compares weight to length directly, without age on the axis, so a short baby and a tall baby can be read on equal terms. When you enter both weight and length, the calculator shows that proportion row in addition to the age-based percentiles. It only appears when both values are present, because the axis is length.
