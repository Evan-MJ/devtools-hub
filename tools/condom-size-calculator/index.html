What Is a Condom Size Calculator?

A condom size calculator converts two body measurements, your erect girth and erect length, into the numbers condom manufacturers actually print on the packet: nominal width in millimetres and product length in millimetres. Instead of asking whether you are small, average or large, it maps your girth onto a starting nominal-width range, shows which standard size band that lands in, and then lets you test any specific condom's published dimensions against your own numbers with an explicit fit verdict. Everything runs locally in your browser, nothing is stored or transmitted, and there is no sign-up or payment in front of the results.

How the Calculator Works

Condom-sizing advice fails most often because it compares the wrong units. A shop label or a chatbot will happily tell you that standard condoms fit most people, but a rolled condom has one measurement that matters, the nominal width, while your body has a different measurement, the circumference. Those two numbers are not the same quantity, which is why the logic here keeps them apart instead of collapsing both into small, medium and large.

The tool works in two directions. Outward: it multiplies your erect girth by 0.40 and by 0.45 to give the low and high ends of a starting nominal-width range, then reports which published band that range sits in, the snug band under 52 mm, the standard band 52 to 56 mm, the large band 56 to 60 mm, or the extra-large band above 60 mm. Inward: you enter the nominal width printed on a wrapper, in millimetres, and the tool computes that condom's circumference, which is about twice its nominal width, then divides it by your girth. As a working window, ratios where the condom's circumference is roughly 80 to 90 percent of your own girth are the ones most likely to sit properly: comfortably snug without fighting the latex. Ratios pushed below about 75 percent stretch the material well past its design tension, which is where pressure, discomfort and breakage live, while a ratio at or above 100 percent leaves the condom sitting as loose as a sleeve, and slippage follows. You get a verdict in those terms, not a vague adjective.

Length has its own, simpler rule. Latex does not stretch lengthwise in any useful way, so a condom shorter than you will stop unrolling before it reaches the base, and a longer condom simply stays rolled at the base, doing no harm. That is why the length check is reported separately instead of being folded into the fit verdict.

The Formula

starting width = 0.40–0.45 × girth  ·  condom circumference ≈ 2 × nominal width  ·  fit ratio = condom circumference ÷ girth

The fit ratio is the whole verdict: inside roughly 0.80 to 0.90 the condom is comfortably snug, below that it stretches hard (breakage territory), at or above 1.0 it sits like a loose sleeve (slippage territory). Enter a product's nominal width and the calculator does both directions for you: your width range outward, that specific product's fit inward.

How to Measure Girth and Length

Measure twice, on different days if you can, and keep both numbers; one reading is rarely as trustworthy as two that agree.

For girth, wrap a soft tape around the widest part of the erect shaft, usually near the middle. The tape should rest against the skin without pressing in. If you have no soft tape, a strip of paper or a length of non-stretch string wrapped the same way works well; mark where the ends overlap, then lay the strip flat against a hard ruler and read it. Do not pad the number and do not round it, because girth is the number that drives the width range entirely.

For length, measure along the top of the shaft, from the pubic bone at the base to the tip, pressing gently inward until the tape stops. The same pressure is what a clinical measurement uses, and it keeps your number comparable to published figures. Erect length varies a little between readings; the calculator treats that as normal, and length only becomes decisive when a specific product's printed length is shorter than what you measured.

Why a Calculator Instead of a Chatbot

You might wonder why this calculator is worth two measurements when any chatbot can answer a question about condom size in a sentence. The honest answer is that chatbots do not compute anything. They produce the statistically likely sentence about condom sizing, which is some version of most condoms are standard and fit most people. That sentence is broadly true at the population level and unhelpful at exactly the resolution a personal question needs, and it cannot take your measured girth plus a package's printed nominal width and tell you whether the two sit in the same window.

This calculator runs deterministic arithmetic. The same inputs always produce the same verdict, the verdict is computed from your numbers rather than sampled from text about people like you, and nothing about the calculation leaves your browser. Measurements of this kind are sensitive, and on this page they never go to a server, never touch a form, and never reach a login. Two measurements, one ratio, zero data collection is the entire deal.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I measure my girth without a soft tape measure?

Wrap a strip of paper, or a piece of non-stretch string, around the erect shaft at its widest point, mark where the end overlaps, then lay the strip flat along a ruler and read the length in whatever units you have. That reading is your circumference. The two common mistakes are holding the strip loosely, which inflates the number, and pulling it tight, which shrinks it: the strip should rest against the skin without indenting it. You can also measure straight across the top of the erect shaft with a flat ruler and multiply by 3.1416, but treat that as a fallback rather than a primary method, since a ruler laid across a tube does not contact the widest point reliably.

What exactly is nominal width?

Nominal width is the width of an unrolled condom laid flat, printed on the packet in millimetres, and it is the one number international standards size a condom by. When the tube lies flat, its circumference is about twice that flat width, so a 56 mm condom wraps roughly 112 mm around. Nominal width is not half your girth: because a condom should sit slightly tighter than you, the starting width this calculator returns, your girth multiplied by about 0.40 to 0.45, comes out a little below half your measured circumference.

My girth sits between two size bands. Which do I buy?

Start at the lower width if you have had slippage, and at the higher one if you have had tightness, discomfort or breakage. Your own failures are data: a condom that rolled up or slipped off was effectively too wide for you no matter what its numbers said, and one that pressed or pinched was too narrow. Buy one condom from each candidate size, often as two singles or a small variety pack, and let one real use settle it, because adjacent bands sit close together and trying one is the cheapest possible test there is.

Does length change my condom size?

Length does not change the width verdict, because the band comes from girth alone. What length decides is whether a condom will unroll all the way to the base. If a product's printed length is shorter than your measured length, that condom will stop unrolling early, and no amount of correct width fixes an over-short tube. Extra length just stays rolled at the base and causes no problem, so among two otherwise equal choices, buy the longer one.

The width matches my numbers, so why does my condom still feel tight?

First check the arithmetic against the packet: the width printed there is the one number that sizes the condom, and if that width sits inside your recommended range, the tension you feel is probably coming from something else. Application error is the most common culprit, a condom not fully unrolled to the base acts narrower and can tear at the tip if the reservoir is not pinched. Shape and thickness matter too, a tapered head or a thicker latex feels tighter at the same nominal width, and no calculator can see those from one number. If two different brands at the same width both feel tight every time, your safe move is the next width band up, not a different technique.

Why should I enter a product's length too?

Together with the width, the printed length tells you whether the condom will unroll fully, so enter it whenever you can read it and see the verdict change before you buy. If a product's length is shorter than your measured length, the calculator warns you instead of letting you find out later, and when the length is fine it says so, which is useful information rather than silence.

Can it recommend a brand?

No, and that is deliberate. Brand line-ups change constantly, a width printed for one product says nothing about the shape or thickness of a different product, and third-party recommendations age badly. What this calculator guarantees is the arithmetic: given any product's printed nominal width and length, you get an honest verdict on how it sits against your measurements. Read the numbers from the packet of whatever you are holding and check them here, and that works for every brand on the shelf, including ones that did not exist when this page was written.

Is anything I type here stored or sent anywhere?

Nothing you type is stored or sent anywhere. The calculation runs in JavaScript inside your browser, with no account, no form and no network call attached to the inputs, and closing the tab erases everything. If you would rather not type the numbers at all, the formulas are printed on the page in plain sight, the nominal width range is your girth times 0.40 to 0.45, the condom circumference is twice its nominal width, and the fit ratio is that circumference divided by your girth, so you can do the arithmetic yourself wherever you are.

Should I re-measure if I get a different number each time?

Yes. Both measurements drift a few millimetres with temperature, arousal level and how much pressure the tape applies, so a single reading is a sample, not a fact. If two readings of the same measurement disagree by more than about 4 mm, take a third and use the middle value. Do not average readings across many days to three decimal places: the output here is a starting band rather than a precision instrument, and a band already absorbs the small variation that real measurements carry.
