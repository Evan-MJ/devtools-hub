How This Heart Rate Zone Calculator Works

Heart rate zones divide the effort between your easiest and your hardest working heartbeat into five bands, and every training plan you have ever seen assumes you know yours. The calculator estimates your maximum heart rate from your age, asks optionally for your resting heart rate, and then turns those two numbers into five bpm ranges you can actually hold: recovery, easy aerobic, tempo, threshold and VO2 max. Enter your age, press Calculate, and the table below fills with numbers rounded to whole beats, so you can glance at your watch mid-run and know exactly which band you are in.

Two methods set where each zone starts and stops. The default uses percentages of your estimated maximum heart rate, the classic model most watches ship with. Type in a resting heart rate and the calculator switches to the Karvonen method, which measures your heart rate reserve, the span between resting and maximum, because a zone that spans 60 to 70 percent of a reserve of 110 beats sits at different bpm values than the same percentages of a raw maximum. Athletes who have measured their true maximum in a lab test can override the estimate entirely, and every formula comparison updates around that number.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are heart rate zones?

Training zones are five bands of heart rate, each anchored to a percentage of your maximum heart rate, that correspond to different physiological jobs. Zone 1 at 50 to 60 percent is active recovery, easy enough to hold a full conversation. Zone 2 at 60 to 70 percent builds aerobic base and is the band most of your weekly volume belongs in. Zone 3 at 70 to 80 percent is tempo work, comfortably hard. Zone 4 at 80 to 90 percent is threshold effort, where talking comes in short phrases. Zone 5 at 90 to 100 percent is maximal work you can only sustain in short bursts. The boundaries are conventions rather than cliff edges, which is why every coach sets them slightly differently.

How do I calculate my maximum heart rate?

The most common estimate is the Fox formula, 220 minus your age, which dates from 1971 and remains the default on many gym machines. Better validated options exist: the Tanaka formula, 208 minus 0.7 times age, came from a meta-analysis of 351 studies and tracks measured maxima more closely across ages; the Gulati formula, 206 minus 0.88 times age, was derived from over five thousand healthy women; and the Nes formula, 211 minus 0.64 times age, comes from a large Norwegian cohort and fits adults over thirty particularly well. The calculator shows what all four give for your age and uses the best-supported one for your sex unless you enter a measured value.

Which max heart rate formula is the most accurate?

None is exact, because maximum heart rate is individual and any formula is a population average with a standard deviation of roughly 10 to 12 beats per minute. At any given age, two healthy people can have maxima 20 beats apart and both be completely normal. That said, the evidence favors Tanaka and Nes over the old 220 minus age rule, and Gulati for women, which is why the calculator defaults to those and treats Fox as the legacy reference. If you have ever seen your true maximum on a monitor during an all-out effort, entering it beats every formula, and the override field exists for exactly that reason.

What is the Karvonen method and when should I use it?

The Karvonen method sets each zone as a percentage of your heart rate reserve, the gap between your resting and maximum heart rate, then adds your resting rate back: target = resting + (maximum - resting) x intensity. It personalizes the zones in a way raw percentages cannot, because two people with the same maximum can have very different resting rates, and the reserve model puts the same relative effort at different bpm values for each. Use it when you know a reliable resting heart rate, ideally measured on waking, and it is the method most running coaches prefer for setting easy and threshold paces by heart rate.

Which zone burns the most fat?

Zone 2, roughly 60 to 70 percent of maximum heart rate, is where the body draws the largest share of its energy from fat, which is why watch makers labeled it the fat burning zone. The label misleads in one important way: the share of energy from fat is highest there, but the total calories burned per minute climbs as intensity rises, so a harder workout can burn more total fat in less time. Zone 2 earns its place in every plan because it builds the aerobic base that makes everything else possible, not because it is uniquely slimming. Pick the zone that matches the session your plan calls for, not the one with the best marketing.

What is Zone 2 and why is everyone talking about it?

Zone 2 is the easy aerobic band, 60 to 70 percent of maximum heart rate, where you can speak in full sentences while running, riding or rowing. It has become the most discussed zone in endurance sport because elite programs spend the large majority of their training time there, and because a decade of research ties it to mitochondrial density, fat metabolism and durability in long events. The practical test is the talk test: if you can hold a conversation, you are in it. Most recreational athletes drift too fast on easy days, which is exactly why the calculator calls your personal Zone 2 bpm range out separately from the table.

How do I measure my resting heart rate?

Measure it in the first minute after waking, before you sit up, ideally on several consecutive mornings and then average them, because a single morning can be skewed by a bad night, alcohol or a hard session the day before. Find your pulse at the wrist or the side of the neck, count the beats in 30 seconds and double it, or let a chest strap or watch do it for you overnight and read the lowest value. Well trained endurance athletes commonly see resting rates in the 40s, average adults sit between 60 and 100, and an unusually high reading that persists for days is worth mentioning to a doctor rather than to a calculator.

Is this calculator medical advice?

No. The numbers it produces are training estimates built on population formulas, and population formulas carry a margin of error wide enough to matter: your true maximum may sit 10 or more beats from any estimate on this page. The zones are a framework for structuring exercise, not a diagnostic, and they do not account for medication such as beta blockers, which cap heart rate and make bpm based zones unreliable, illness, heat or altitude. If you have a heart condition, are pregnant, or are starting exercise after a long sedentary period, set your zones with a clinician or a supervised test rather than with a web page.
